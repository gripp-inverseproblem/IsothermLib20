Temkin isotherm

Original publication and scope

The Temkin isotherm is associated with the treatment of adsorption on energetically non-uniform surfaces and interactions between adsorbed molecules. The frequently cited Temkin–Pyzhev paper (1940) examines ammonia-synthesis kinetics, whereas Temkin (1941) directly addresses adsorption equilibrium. The two publications should not be treated as interchangeable sources for a particular isotherm equation.

Temkin, M. I., and Pyzhev, V. (1940), Kinetics of ammonia synthesis on promoted iron catalysts, Acta Physicochimica URSS, 12, 327–356. Bibliographic record.

Temkin, M. I. (1941), Adsorption equilibrium and the kinetics of processes on non-uniform surfaces and in the interaction between adsorbed molecules, Zhurnal Fizicheskoi Khimii, 15, 296–332. Bibliographic record.

Johnson, R. D., and Arnold, F. H. (1995), The Temkin isotherm describes heterogeneous protein adsorption, Biochimica et Biophysica Acta, 1247 (2), 293–297. DOI: 10.1016/0167-4838(95)00006-G. This is an example of a subsequent application to protein adsorption.

Chu, K. H. (2021), Revisiting the Temkin Isotherm: Dimensional Inconsistency and Approximate Forms, Industrial & Engineering Chemistry Research, 60 (35), 13140–13147. DOI: 10.1021/acs.iecr.1c01788. Chu examines the inconsistent dimensions and approximate nature of Temkin expressions commonly used for aqueous adsorption, including their limiting behaviour.

Salvestrini, S., Ambrosone, L., and Kopinke, F.-D. (2022), Some mistakes and misinterpretations in the analysis of thermodynamic adsorption data, Journal of Molecular Liquids, 352, 118762. DOI: 10.1016/j.molliq.2022.118762. This paper discusses thermodynamic and dimensional consistency, uncertainty in fitted parameters and the assumptions underlying adsorption models, including Temkin.

Chu, K. H., Hashim, M. A., Hayder, G., and Bollinger, J.-C. (2024), Rooting out faulty adsorption models. Comment on “Magnetic Prussian blue nanoshells are controllable (sic) anchored on the surface of molybdenum disulfide nanosheets for efficient separation of radioactive cesium from water”, Science of the Total Environment, 921, 171118. DOI: 10.1016/j.scitotenv.2024.171118. This correspondence includes the Temkin isotherm among the models considered in its critique of an individual adsorption study.

Mudhoo, A. (2025), Unveiling new insights: Revised Temkin adsorption isotherm parameters from fresh curve fits in adsorption studies, Chemical Engineering Science, 311, 121585. DOI: 10.1016/j.ces.2025.121585. This study refits published adsorption data using the alternative nonlinear Temkin expression discussed by Chu (2021) and reports differences in estimated parameters.

The C++ class described below implements the logarithmic expression \(q_e=(RT/b_T)\ln(K_TC_e)\), not the alternative form investigated by Chu (2021) and Mudhoo (2025). The logarithm requires \(K_TC_e\) to be dimensionless, but this condition alone does not establish the dimensional consistency of the loading: the prefactor \(RT/b_T\) must also have units consistent with \(q_e\). In particular, if \(b_T\) is an energy per mole, \(RT/b_T\) is dimensionless. The implemented expression has neither a linear low-concentration limit nor a finite saturation limit. The historical papers supply context and must not be read as verification of the particular parameter convention used in this class.

Mathematical formulation

Temperature must always be supplied in kelvin (K) as an independent thermodynamic input, not an adjustable adsorption parameter.

\[q_e(C_e,T)=\frac{RT}{b_T}\ln(K_TC_e).\]

The admissible parameter and input domain is

\[K_T>0,\quad b_T>0,\quad T>0\ \mathrm{K},\quad C_e>0,\quad K_TC_e\geq1.\]

The gas constant uses the units selected for the model, and \(b_T\) must use compatible energy units. Temperature is always in kelvin.

Thermal two-parameter Temkin model, evaluated at a supplied absolute temperature; temperature does not count as a fitted parameter.

Analytical concentration derivative

\[\frac{\partial q_e}{\partial C_e}=\frac{RT}{b_T C_e}.\]

C++20 usage

#include <isotherm/isotherm.hpp>

const isotherm::models::Temkin model{4.0, 1800.0};
const isotherm::Real temperature_k = 298.15;                                   // Always in kelvin.
const isotherm::Real uptake = isotherm::evaluate(model, 1.0, temperature_k);   // 1.9092023
const isotherm::Real slope = isotherm::derivative(model, 1.0, temperature_k);  // 1.3771983

// Select kJ/(mol K) for R; convert b_T from J to kJ consistently.
const isotherm::models::Temkin model_kj{
    4.0, 1.8, isotherm::gas_constant_unit::kilojoule_per_mole_kelvin};
const isotherm::Real same_uptake =
    isotherm::evaluate(model_kj, 1.0, temperature_k);                          // 1.9092023

Reproducible uptake curve

Linearised relationship

\[q_e=\frac{RT}{b_T}\ln K_T+\frac{RT}{b_T}\ln C_e.\]

Numerical sensitivity

For each model coefficient \(k_i\), the scaled sensitivity at fixed temperature is

\[\lambda_i(C_e,T)=k_i\frac{\partial q_e(C_e,T)}{\partial k_i}.\]

Numerical values are obtained by perturbing individual parameters and evaluating new instances of the C++ model. The accompanying C++20 example performs this procedure.

Validation and limitations

\[K_T>0,\quad b_T>0,\quad T>0\ \mathrm{K},\quad C_e>0,\quad K_TC_e\geq1.\]
